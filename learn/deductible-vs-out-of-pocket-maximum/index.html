Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum, explained

Two numbers on every health plan, and the difference between them can change what a bad year costs you.

Last updated: November 1, 2026 · By Suzelaine Similien, Fern & Fine Print

  1. Home
  2. Learn
  3. Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum, explained

The short answer

Your deductible is what you pay for covered care before your plan starts paying its share. Your out-of-pocket maximum is the most you'll pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year; after you reach it, the plan pays 100% of covered costs for the rest of that year. Your deductible, copays, and coinsurance all count toward the out-of-pocket maximum. Your monthly premiums don't. (HealthCare.gov: deductible · out-of-pocket maximum)

What is a deductible?

A deductible is the amount you pay for covered health care services before your insurance plan starts to pay. HealthCare.gov's example: with a $2,000 deductible, you pay the first $2,000 of covered services yourself. After that, you usually pay only a copay or coinsurance and the plan pays the rest. (HealthCare.gov)

Two helpful exceptions:

  • Marketplace plans cover certain preventive services, like many screenings and vaccines, at no cost when you use an in-network provider, even before you meet your deductible.
  • Many plans pay for some services, like certain office visits, before the deductible. Some plans have a separate deductible for things like prescription drugs. Your plan details tell you which applies.

What is an out-of-pocket maximum?

The out-of-pocket maximum (also called the out-of-pocket limit) is the most you have to pay for covered services in a plan year. Once what you've spent on deductibles, copays, and coinsurance for in-network care reaches that amount, the plan pays 100% of covered benefits for the rest of the plan year. (HealthCare.gov)

According to HealthCare.gov, for 2027 a Marketplace plan's out-of-pocket limit can be no more than $12,000 for an individual or $24,000 for a family. Many plans set theirs lower; your plan's own number is in its Summary of Benefits and Coverage.

What doesn't count toward the out-of-pocket maximum?

HealthCare.gov lists these as not included:

  • Your monthly premiums
  • Anything you spend on services your plan doesn't cover
  • Out-of-network care and services
  • Costs above the plan's allowed amount that a provider may charge you (see balance billing)

How do they work together? A worked example

Here's a made-up plan, just to show the math. It is not a real plan and not a suggestion.

Example plan (hypothetical)
Deductible$2,000
Coinsurance after the deductible20%
Out-of-pocket maximum$6,000
  1. A $10,000 covered, in-network hospital bill (after the plan's allowed amount). You pay the first $2,000 (your deductible). Then you pay 20% of the remaining $8,000, which is $1,600. Your total so far: $3,600.
  2. Later that year, a $30,000 covered, in-network bill. Your 20% would be $6,000, but you only have $2,400 left before you reach your $6,000 out-of-pocket maximum. So you pay $2,400.
  3. For the rest of that plan year, the plan pays 100% of covered, in-network care. Your premiums are still due every month.
The takeaway: the deductible tells you when the plan starts sharing costs. The out-of-pocket maximum tells you the most a really hard year could cost you for covered, in-network care, on top of your premiums.

Which number matters more when I compare plans?

Both, plus the premium. HealthCare.gov suggests comparing your estimated total yearly cost for each plan, not just the monthly premium: premiums for 12 months, plus what you'd likely pay when you get care, with the out-of-pocket maximum as the ceiling. (HealthCare.gov: your total costs) Our guide to comparing two plans side by side shows a simple way to lay that out.

Where do copays and coinsurance fit in?

A copayment is a fixed amount, like $20 for a visit. Coinsurance is a percentage, like 20% of the allowed amount. Both usually apply after your deductible (some copays apply before it), and both count toward your out-of-pocket maximum. See the health insurance terms glossary for more.

Quick questions

What is the difference between a deductible and an out-of-pocket maximum?

The deductible is what you pay for covered care before your plan starts sharing costs. The out-of-pocket maximum is the most you pay for covered, in-network care in a plan year; after you reach it, the plan pays 100% of covered costs for the rest of the year.

Does my deductible count toward my out-of-pocket maximum?

Yes. Spending on your deductible, copays, and coinsurance for covered, in-network care all counts toward the out-of-pocket maximum.

Do premiums count toward the out-of-pocket maximum?

No. Monthly premiums don't count toward the deductible or the out-of-pocket maximum.

What is the 2027 out-of-pocket maximum for Marketplace plans?

According to HealthCare.gov, for 2027 a Marketplace plan's out-of-pocket limit can be no more than $12,000 for an individual or $24,000 for a family. Many plans set a lower limit.

Is preventive care free before I meet my deductible?

Marketplace plans must cover certain preventive services at no cost when you use an in-network provider, even before you meet your deductible. Coverage may vary, so check your plan.
