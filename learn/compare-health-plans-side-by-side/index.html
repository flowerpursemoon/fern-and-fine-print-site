How to compare two health plans side by side

A plain, pen-and-paper way to line two plans up so the differences actually show.

Last updated: November 1, 2026 · By Suzelaine Similien, Fern & Fine Print

  1. Home
  2. Learn
  3. How to compare two health plans side by side

The short answer

Compare plans on total yearly cost, not just the monthly premium: 12 months of premiums plus what you'd likely pay for care, with the out-of-pocket maximum as your worst-case ceiling. Then check that your doctors are in network, your drugs are on the formulary, and the plan type (HMO, PPO, EPO, POS) fits how you get care. Each plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage puts these numbers in a standard format. (HealthCare.gov: your total costs · SBC)

What should I compare first?

Start with the two things that can rule a plan out completely: are my doctors in network, and are my prescriptions covered? If a plan fails either, the price may not matter. Our guide on checking doctors and drugs walks through it.

How do I estimate my total yearly cost?

HealthCare.gov suggests comparing estimated total yearly costs: monthly premium × 12, plus deductibles, copays, and coinsurance, with the out-of-pocket maximum as the most you'd spend on covered care. (HealthCare.gov) An easy way is to work out two scenarios for each plan:

  • A quiet year: premiums × 12, plus a few visits and your regular prescriptions.
  • A hard year: premiums × 12, plus the out-of-pocket maximum.

Here's a made-up example to show the math. These are not real plans, and the numbers aren't a prediction or a recommendation.

Worked example (hypothetical plans)
Plan APlan B
Monthly premium$300$450
Premiums for 12 months$3,600$5,400
Out-of-pocket maximum$8,000$4,000
Quiet year (premiums + about $200 of visits)about $3,800about $5,600
Hard year (premiums + out-of-pocket maximum)$11,600$9,400

Neither plan "wins." Plan A costs less in a quiet year; Plan B caps a hard year lower. Seeing both scenarios helps you decide which tradeoff fits your health, your budget, and your nerves. (Remember that out-of-network care and services the plan doesn't cover sit outside the out-of-pocket maximum.)

What do Bronze, Silver, Gold, and Platinum mean?

Metal levels show how you and the plan share costs, and they have nothing to do with quality of care. HealthCare.gov's estimates of the plan's share of costs for covered services: Bronze about 60%, Silver about 70%, Gold about 80%, Platinum about 90%. Bronze plans generally have higher deductibles and Gold and Platinum lower ones. Actual costs vary by plan. (HealthCare.gov)

Two details: if you qualify for cost-sharing reductions (extra savings) based on income, you get them only with a Silver plan. A premium tax credit, if you qualify, can be used with a plan in any metal category.

HMO, PPO, EPO, or POS: which plan type fits?

  • HMO: usually covers only in-network care except emergencies; often needs referrals.
  • EPO: covers only in-network care except emergencies.
  • POS: you pay less in network and need a referral to see a specialist.
  • PPO: you pay less in network but can go out of network without a referral, for an additional cost.

(HealthCare.gov, plan and network types) Our free course has a short lesson on HMO vs PPO.

Should I care whether a plan is HSA-eligible?

Only if you want to use a Health Savings Account. You can contribute to an HSA only if you have an HSA-eligible (high deductible) plan. HealthCare.gov notes that all Bronze and Catastrophic plans work with HSAs, and some plans in other categories do too. (HealthCare.gov)

Quick questions

How do I compare health insurance plans?

Compare total yearly cost (12 months of premiums plus likely out-of-pocket costs, with the out-of-pocket maximum as a ceiling), then confirm your doctors are in network, your drugs are covered, and the plan type fits how you get care.

What is the difference between Bronze and Silver plans?

They share costs differently. HealthCare.gov estimates a Bronze plan pays about 60% of covered costs and a Silver plan about 70%. Bronze plans generally have higher deductibles. Cost-sharing reductions are only available with Silver plans.

Do metal levels mean better quality care?

No. HealthCare.gov says the metal categories have nothing to do with the quality of care; they show how you and the plan share costs.

Is the cheapest premium the best plan?

Not necessarily. A low premium often comes with a higher deductible or out-of-pocket maximum, so it helps to compare total yearly cost in both a quiet year and a hard year.

Where do I find a plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage?

You get an SBC when you shop for coverage, renew, or change plans, and you can request one from the insurance company. Marketplace plan details link to it.
