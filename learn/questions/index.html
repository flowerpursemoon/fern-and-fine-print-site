Open Enrollment and deadlines

When is Open Enrollment for 2027?

For states that use HealthCare.gov, Open Enrollment for 2027 Marketplace coverage runs from November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027. States that run their own marketplace can set different dates, so check your state's official site.

Official source: HealthCare.gov dates and deadlines · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 7, Your next step

What is the last day to sign up for coverage starting January 1?

On HealthCare.gov, it's December 15, 2026. If you enroll from December 16 through January 15, coverage starts February 1. Either way, coverage begins only after you pay your first premium.

Official source: HealthCare.gov dates and deadlines · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 7, Your next step

Does my state have a different Open Enrollment deadline?

It might. Some states run their own marketplace instead of HealthCare.gov and can set their own deadlines. HealthCare.gov's state page tells you where people in your state apply; for example, Oregon residents use ExploreHealthOR.gov for 2027 coverage.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, The Marketplace in your state · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 7, Your next step

Can I change my health plan after Open Enrollment?

Generally only if you qualify for a Special Enrollment Period because of a life event, like losing other coverage, moving, getting married, or having or adopting a baby. You can apply for Medicaid or CHIP any time.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Special Enrollment Period · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 2, When work insurance gets expensive (or ends)

What happens if I miss Open Enrollment?

Outside Open Enrollment, you can enroll in or change a Marketplace plan only with a Special Enrollment Period. Depending on the type, you usually have 60 days before or after a qualifying life event. Job-based plans and Medicare have their own rules.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Special Enrollment Period · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 2, When work insurance gets expensive (or ends)

What is a Special Enrollment Period?

It's a time outside Open Enrollment when you can sign up for coverage because of certain life events, such as losing coverage, moving, marriage, or a new child. You usually have 60 days before or after the event, and job-based plans must offer at least 30 days.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 2, When work insurance gets expensive (or ends)

Will I be automatically re-enrolled in my Marketplace plan?

Many people are if they don't act by December 15, but not everyone. Your Open Enrollment letters say which plan, if any, you'd be re-enrolled in, and HealthCare.gov recommends updating your application so your savings are figured correctly.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Keep or change your plan · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Lesson 7, Your next step

When is Medicare Open Enrollment?

Medicare's Open Enrollment Period runs October 15 to December 7 each year, with changes taking effect January 1. People already in a Medicare Advantage Plan also have a separate window from January 1 to March 31.

Official source: Medicare.gov, Joining a plan · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

When is open enrollment at work?

Your employer sets its own open enrollment window, and it may not match the Marketplace dates. Your HR or benefits office can tell you the exact dates.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Open Enrollment 2027: key dates · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Can I apply for Medicaid any time of year?

Yes. According to HealthCare.gov, you can apply for Medicaid or the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) any time, and if you qualify, coverage can begin right away.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Free course: Lesson 7, Your next step

Costs and coverage words

What is a premium?

Your premium is the amount you pay for health insurance every month, whether or not you use care. You usually also have other costs when you get care, like a deductible, copays, and coinsurance.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What is a deductible in health insurance?

A deductible is what you pay for covered care before your plan starts to pay its share. With a $2,000 deductible, you pay the first $2,000 of covered services; after that you usually pay a copay or coinsurance.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What does out-of-pocket maximum mean?

It's the most you pay for covered, in-network services in a plan year. After your deductibles, copays, and coinsurance reach it, the plan pays 100% of covered costs for the rest of that year. Premiums don't count toward it.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What's the difference between a deductible and an out-of-pocket maximum?

The deductible is when your plan starts sharing costs; the out-of-pocket maximum is the ceiling on what you pay for covered, in-network care in a year. Money you spend on your deductible counts toward the out-of-pocket maximum.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What is the 2027 out-of-pocket maximum?

According to HealthCare.gov, for 2027 a Marketplace plan's out-of-pocket limit can be no more than $12,000 for an individual or $24,000 for a family. Many plans set lower limits; check the plan's Summary of Benefits and Coverage.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is a copay?

A copay (copayment) is a fixed amount, like $20, that you pay for a covered service. Copays can be different for different services within the same plan, and some apply only after the deductible.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What is coinsurance?

Coinsurance is your share of a covered service's cost as a percentage, like 20%, usually after you've paid your deductible. If the allowed amount is $100 and your coinsurance is 20%, you'd pay $20.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

What's the difference between a copay and coinsurance?

A copay is a flat dollar amount per service. Coinsurance is a percentage of the allowed amount. Both count toward your out-of-pocket maximum.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

Does my premium count toward my deductible?

No. Premiums are what you pay to keep the plan; they don't count toward your deductible or your out-of-pocket maximum.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Lesson 1, What “coverage” actually means

Is preventive care free?

Most health plans, including Marketplace plans, must cover a set of preventive services like certain screenings and vaccines at no cost when you use an in-network provider, even before you meet your deductible. HealthCare.gov notes coverage may vary.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Preventive health services · Read more: Deductible vs out-of-pocket maximum · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is an allowed amount?

It's the most a plan will pay for a covered service, also called the negotiated rate. If a provider charges more, you may have to pay the difference.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is balance billing?

Balance billing is when a provider bills you for the difference between their charge and your plan's allowed amount. An in-network (preferred) provider may not balance bill you for covered services.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is an Explanation of Benefits (EOB)?

An EOB is a statement from your health plan after you get care, showing what was billed, what the plan paid, and what you may owe. It isn't a bill, but it's useful to compare against any bill you receive.

Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is a Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC)?

An SBC is a standard, easy-to-read summary of a plan's costs and coverage, designed for apples-to-apples comparisons. You get one when you shop, renew, or change coverage, and you can request one from the insurer.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Choosing and comparing plans

How do I compare health insurance plans?

Compare total yearly cost (12 months of premiums plus likely out-of-pocket costs, with the out-of-pocket maximum as the ceiling), not just the premium. Then confirm your doctors are in network, your drugs are covered, and the plan type fits how you get care.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Your total costs · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Lesson 3, Affordable vs “covers everything” + checklist

How do I choose a health insurance plan?

Start with your must-keep doctors and regular prescriptions, then compare plans that cover them on total yearly cost and plan type. HealthCare.gov's plan-choice guide covers metal levels, total costs, and network types.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, How to choose a plan · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Lesson 3, Affordable vs “covers everything” + checklist

What's the difference between Bronze and Silver plans?

They share costs differently. HealthCare.gov estimates a Bronze plan pays about 60% of covered costs and a Silver plan about 70%, and Bronze deductibles are generally higher. If you qualify for extra savings (cost-sharing reductions), you get them only with a Silver plan.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Health plan categories · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What's the difference between Silver and Gold plans?

HealthCare.gov estimates a Silver plan pays about 70% of covered costs and a Gold plan about 80%. Gold plans generally have lower deductibles and higher premiums; actual costs vary by plan.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Health plan categories · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Do metal levels mean better quality care?

No. HealthCare.gov says the categories have nothing to do with the quality of care; they show how you and the plan split costs for covered services.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Health plan categories · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What's the difference between an HMO and a PPO?

An HMO usually covers only in-network care except emergencies and often requires referrals. A PPO lets you see out-of-network providers without a referral, for an additional cost. Neither is better for everyone; a network mismatch is what hurts.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Plan and network types · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Lesson 4, HMO vs PPO in one coffee

What are EPO and POS plans?

An EPO covers services only from in-network providers, except in emergencies. A POS plan charges less when you stay in network and requires a referral from your primary care doctor to see a specialist.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Plan and network types · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Lesson 4, HMO vs PPO in one coffee

What is a catastrophic health plan?

A catastrophic plan has a lower premium and higher out-of-pocket costs, and covers 3 primary care visits a year before the deductible, plus preventive care. It's available to people under 30 or people who qualify for a hardship or affordability exemption.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Lesson 5, Pre-existing conditions & plan types that surprise people

What is a premium tax credit?

It's a tax credit that can lower your monthly Marketplace premium, based on your household and estimated income. If you use more in advance than you qualify for based on your final income, you may have to pay back the difference at tax time.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What are cost-sharing reductions?

Cost-sharing reductions, often called extra savings, lower your deductibles, copays, and coinsurance if you qualify based on income. In the Marketplace, you get them only if you enroll in a Silver plan.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Comparing two plans side by side · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is an HSA?

A Health Savings Account lets you set aside pre-tax money for qualified medical expenses like deductibles and copays. You can contribute only if you have an HSA-eligible (high deductible) plan, and HSA funds generally can't pay premiums.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is a high deductible health plan?

An HDHP has a higher deductible and usually a lower premium, and it can be paired with an HSA, which is why it's often called an HSA-eligible plan. HealthCare.gov says all Bronze and Catastrophic Marketplace plans work with HSAs.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Can a plan turn me down for a pre-existing condition?

ACA-compliant health plans, including Marketplace plans, can't refuse to cover you or charge you more because of a pre-existing condition. Some short-term or other non-ACA products can exclude conditions you already have.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, Pre-existing conditions · Free course: Lesson 5, Pre-existing conditions & plan types that surprise people

Doctors, drugs, and bills

How do I know if my doctor is in network?

Search the provider directory for the exact plan name and year, then confirm by calling your doctor's office and the plan. Write down the date, who you spoke with, and any reference number.

Official source: CMS, What you should know about provider networks (PDF) · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Lesson 3, Affordable vs “covers everything” + checklist

Is my medication covered by my insurance?

Look up each drug in the plan's formulary (drug list) for the year you're enrolling in, and note its tier and any prior authorization, step therapy, or quantity limits. Then confirm with the plan and your pharmacy.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary: formulary · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Lesson 3, Affordable vs “covers everything” + checklist

What is a formulary?

A formulary is the list of prescription drugs a plan covers, also called a drug list. Drugs are often grouped into tiers with different costs.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is prior authorization?

Prior authorization is approval a plan may require before you get a service or fill a prescription in order for it to be covered. Your doctor's office usually submits the request.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

What is a referral?

A referral is a written order from your primary care doctor to see a specialist or get certain services. In many HMOs, the plan may not pay for specialist care without one.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Lesson 4, HMO vs PPO in one coffee

What protects me from surprise medical bills?

According to CMS, the No Surprises Act protects people with most types of health insurance from unexpected out-of-network bills for emergency room visits, certain non-emergency care related to a visit to an in-network hospital or surgery center, and air ambulance services.

Official source: CMS, Medical bill rights · Read more: Checking doctors and drugs · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Job changes, Medicare, and Medicaid

What happens to my health insurance if I lose my job?

Losing job-based coverage, for any reason, generally qualifies you for a Special Enrollment Period to get a Marketplace plan; you need to apply within 60 days. COBRA may also let you keep your job plan for a while.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, If you lose job-based coverage · Free course: Lesson 2, When work insurance gets expensive (or ends)

What is COBRA insurance?

COBRA can let you keep your job-based health plan for a limited time after you leave. You usually pay the full premium yourself, including the part your employer used to pay.

Official source: HealthCare.gov, If you lose job-based coverage · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Lesson 2, When work insurance gets expensive (or ends)

What's the difference between Medicare and Medicaid?

Medicare is federal health insurance for people 65 and older, certain younger people with disabilities, and people with End-Stage Renal Disease. Medicaid is a joint federal and state program that provides free or low-cost coverage to some people with low incomes and others; rules vary by state.

Official source: HealthCare.gov glossary: Medicare · Read more: Health insurance terms glossary · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Where can I get free, unbiased Medicare help?

Every state has a State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) that offers free, personalized Medicare counseling. Medicare.gov notes that SHIPs aren't connected to any insurance company or plan.

Official source: SHIP national site · Free course: Insurance Without the Headache

Life insurance basics

What's the difference between health insurance and life insurance?

Health insurance helps pay for medical care while you're living. Life insurance pays money to the people you choose (your beneficiaries) if you die while the policy is in force. A Marketplace health plan doesn't include life insurance.

Official source: CMS FAQ (PDF) · Free course: Lesson 6, Life insurance and your health coverage

What's the difference between term and whole life insurance?

Term life covers a set period, like 10, 20, or 30 years, and ends when the term ends unless you extend it. Whole life is a type of permanent insurance meant to last your entire life if you keep it, usually with higher premiums at first.

Official source: Texas Department of Insurance life insurance guide · Free course: Lesson 6, Life insurance and your health coverage

Does work life insurance end when I leave my job?

Group life through work typically ends when the job ends, and COBRA doesn't continue life-only coverage. Ask your benefits office about converting or porting it before your last day.

Official source: U.S. Department of Labor, COBRA guide · Free course: Lesson 6, Life insurance and your health coverage

How do I think about whether I need life insurance?

A common starting question is who would need financial help if your income stopped. The Insurance Information Institute describes a needs-based way to think about how much coverage, if any, fits your situation.

Official source: Insurance Information Institute · Free course: Lesson 6, Life insurance and your health coverage
